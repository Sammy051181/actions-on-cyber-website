Daily SMB Cyber Intelligence Brief

Ransomware affiliates target remote access gear; AI-boosted phishing raises the bar

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Monday 10 August 2026, 18:07 UK time
Today’s look-out: Ransomware entry via remote access appliances + convincing AI-assisted phishing

What to look out for today

Two themes to brief staff and sanity-check with IT today:

  • Ransomware groups actively exploiting remote access appliances (especially VPN / remote access portals) as a way into organisations.
  • More convincing phishing and social engineering as attackers use AI to write better emails, create believable pretexts, and move faster.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

  • SMEs often rely on remote access to run the business (outsourced IT, remote working, third-party support). If that front door is weak, attackers can move quickly to disruption.
  • Ransomware is no longer just about encrypting files—many crews use double extortion (steal data, then threaten to publish it), which increases legal, regulatory and reputational impact.
  • “Looks legitimate” is no longer a useful test for emails and calls. AI makes scams more polished, more targeted, and more persistent.

Warning signs

  • Unexpected MFA prompts, “approve sign-in” requests, or password reset emails.
  • New or unusual remote logins (odd times, unfamiliar locations/devices) or complaints that remote access is slow/unreliable.
  • Emails or calls pushing urgency: invoice/payment changes, payroll updates, “account will be closed”, “security check required”.
  • Staff being asked to install an update, “new VPN”, “remote support tool”, or to open a file to “verify” something.
  • Backups suddenly failing, or lots of files changing at once without an obvious business reason.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • Exploit weaknesses in internet-facing remote access systems (VPN / remote access appliances) to gain an initial foothold.
  • Steal credentials using phishing, then log in through legitimate portals, blending in with normal activity.
  • Use AI-assisted social engineering to persuade someone to approve an MFA prompt, share a one-time code, or change payment details.
  • Move to data theft + encryption to maximise pressure, targeting shared drives and business-critical systems.

What to do today

  • Confirm what remote access you have (VPN, remote access portal, remote support tools) and who administers it (internal IT/MSP/vendor).
  • Re-brief staff: never approve unexpected MFA prompts; never share one-time codes; verify payment or payroll changes using a known phone number.
  • Check admin access: reduce who has admin rights; ensure admins use separate admin accounts and strong MFA.
  • Backups check: verify last successful backup and that you can restore a sample file (not just that “backup ran”).
  • Prepare a 30-minute disruption plan: who calls IT/MSP, who speaks to customers, how to keep taking payments, and where key contacts are stored offline.

Ask your IT provider

  • Do we have any internet-facing remote access appliances? If yes, who monitors them and how quickly are security fixes applied?
  • Do you have alerting for unusual remote logins and repeated MFA prompts?
  • Can you confirm our backup restore test results from the last 30 days?
  • What’s our ransomware playbook—who does what in the first hour, and how do you isolate affected machines quickly?
  • Do you use device-based controls (trusted devices / conditional access) so stolen passwords alone are less useful?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

CISA is warning about ransomware activity and has highlighted exploitation of remote access technology (including VPN-style entry points). If your business (or your MSP) runs remote access appliances, treat this as a priority operational check: confirm what is exposed to the internet, that updates are applied promptly, and that logs/alerts are being watched for unusual access.

One action today

Phone your IT/MSP today and ask them to confirm (in plain English) what remote access/VPN systems you have, whether any are exposed to the internet, and what monitoring is in place for unusual logins and MFA prompt-bombing.
