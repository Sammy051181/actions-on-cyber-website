Daily SMB Cyber Intelligence Brief

Today’s SME cyber look‑out: “Passkey/SSO” phishing targeting Microsoft 365 accounts

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Saturday 12 September 2026, 12:36 UK time
Today’s look-out: Microsoft 365 login scams and data theft (passkey/SSO themed)

What to look out for today

Be on alert for phishing emails, calls or chat messages that claim you need to “set up a passkey”, “upgrade sign‑in”, “reconfirm SSO”, or “fix Microsoft 365 access”. Reports say these social‑engineering lures are being used to take over Microsoft accounts and steal data from Microsoft 365 services.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

For many SMEs, Microsoft 365 is the backbone for email, files and day‑to‑day operations. If one staff account is tricked into handing over access, attackers may be able to read emails, access documents, download contact lists, and use the compromised mailbox to impersonate your business (for invoice scams or further phishing).

Warning signs

  • Messages urging you to “enable passkeys now” or warning of an “account lockout” unless you act immediately.
  • Unexpected sign‑in prompts, approval requests, or “security upgrade” steps you weren’t expecting.
  • Emails that look like Microsoft but use unusual wording, poor formatting, or pressure tactics.
  • Staff reporting they can’t access files/email and were told to follow a link to “restore access”.
  • New mailbox rules, forwarding set up, or unusual sent items reported by users.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • Credential capture / social engineering: trick staff into entering details on a fake Microsoft sign‑in page or approving a sign‑in they didn’t initiate.
  • Account takeover: once in, attackers can search email threads for invoices, bank details, payroll, customer data or contracts.
  • Business email compromise (BEC): attackers can reply within real email chains to request payment changes or divert invoices.
  • Data theft and extortion: downloaded files or emails may later be used to pressure the organisation.

What to do today

  • Send a short staff warning: “We are not asking anyone to ‘set up passkeys’ via email links. If you get a sign‑in/security upgrade message, report it.”
  • Move sign‑in changes to a trusted route: staff should only change sign‑in settings by typing the Microsoft 365 portal address themselves or using a known bookmark.
  • Prioritise your highest‑risk mailboxes: finance, payroll, HR, senior leadership, shared inboxes.
  • Check for mailbox tampering: look for new forwarding rules, suspicious inbox rules, and unusual “consent” or sign‑in activity.
  • Rehearse the first 30 minutes: who to call (IT/MSP), how to lock an account quickly, and how to pause payments if finance mailboxes are affected.

Ask your IT provider

  • Can you show us our recent Microsoft 365 sign‑in alerts and whether any risky sign‑ins occurred this week?
  • Do we have strong sign‑in protections in place for Microsoft 365 (especially for finance/admin accounts), and are alerts monitored?
  • What is our process to rapidly disable a user, reset sessions, and remove suspicious mailbox rules if an account is compromised?
  • Do we have an agreed process to verify payment detail changes (out-of-band verification) if a mailbox is suspected compromised?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

Some reporting also notes active attacks against self-hosted JFrog Artifactory servers. If you or your suppliers run developer tooling internally, confirm with your IT/provider whether you use Artifactory and whether it is internet-exposed—this is more of a supplier/IT estate question than an end-user patching task.

One action today

Send a same-day internal message telling staff to ignore any “passkey/SSO setup” links and to report unexpected Microsoft sign-in prompts immediately—especially anyone in finance/payroll.
