Today’s SMB Cyber Brief: Phishing using remote‑management tools, AI lures, and leaked logins

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Wednesday 30 September 2026, 21:48 UK time
Today’s look-out: Phishing that installs remote-management tools + AI-themed malware lures

What to look out for today

1) Phishing that installs legitimate remote-management software (so attackers can control a PC remotely). Campaigns are using meeting invites, PDF-themed emails and “software update” prompts to trick staff into running an installer.

2) AI / “ChatGPT Custom GPT” themed lures that direct users to malicious sites and attempt to get them to “fix” something by clicking through steps, leading to malware installation.

3) Leaked credentials risk: large numbers of passwords/API keys are still being found exposed in public code repositories, and many remain valid—meaning criminals can log straight into business systems if any of your secrets are exposed.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

  • RMM misuse often looks like normal IT support. If an attacker gets a remote tool installed, they can browse files, capture credentials, move money via finance systems, or deploy ransomware—often without needing to “hack” anything sophisticated.
  • AI-branded scams increase click-through because staff assume anything “ChatGPT/AI” is modern, trusted, and safe to try.
  • Credential leaks don’t just hit tech firms. Marketing tools, accounting integrations, website admin panels, payment providers, and email services all rely on secrets that sometimes end up copied into shared folders, scripts, or code.

Warning signs

  • Unexpected “meeting invite”, “shared PDF”, or “document viewer” prompts that ask you to download and run something.
  • Pop-ups or emails claiming you must install an update urgently (especially if it’s not from your usual IT channel).
  • Any request to approve remote access, install “monitoring”, or allow a tool you don’t recognise (e.g., a new support agent).
  • Staff saying they found a “Custom GPT” or AI tool that asks them to click links outside the platform to “enable”, “fix”, or “verify” something.
  • Unexplained new software appearing on PCs, or users seeing remote-control behaviour (cursor moving, windows opening).

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • Dual-RMM foothold: they trick a user into installing a legitimate remote-management tool, then use it to deploy additional remote-access software for resilience.
  • Business email and finance abuse: once inside, they look for invoice runs, supplier payments, saved bank details, payroll files, and email rules.
  • Credential harvesting: they capture browser-stored passwords or tokens, then pivot into Microsoft 365, accounting, CRM, and file-sharing.
  • Supply-chain style entry: exposed keys/passwords from public repositories can grant access to cloud services, storage buckets, email APIs, website admin, or automation tools—leading to data access or account takeover.

What to do today

  • Send a 2-minute staff warning: “No installing software from meeting invites/PDF prompts/‘updates’. If in doubt, forward to IT.”
  • Lock down software installs where possible (standard user accounts, admin prompts controlled by IT, application allow-listing if you have it).
  • Review remote access policy: who is allowed to use remote tools, which products are approved, and how support sessions are requested/authorised.
  • Turn on stronger sign-in controls for key systems (M365, email, finance, payroll): MFA everywhere and alerts for new device sign-ins.
  • Secret/credential hygiene: if you have any in-house development or website support, scan repos for exposed keys and rotate anything sensitive immediately.

Ask your IT provider

  • Which remote-management tools are approved for our business, and how do you prevent unauthorised ones being installed?
  • Do we have alerting for new remote-control software installs and unusual remote sessions?
  • Can you restrict staff from installing software without approval (and what’s the impact on day-to-day work)?
  • How quickly can you isolate a single PC if we suspect remote takeover?
  • Do you monitor for exposed credentials (e.g., in public repositories) linked to our domains and services, and what is our rotation process?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

Today’s main risk is social engineering and remote access misuse, but keep an eye on perimeter devices and routers: if your business uses MikroTik RouterOS or Citrix NetScaler (common in some MSP and hosted setups), ask your IT provider to confirm their patch and monitoring status and whether any suspicious activity was seen in September.

One action today

Email staff today: “Do not install software from meeting invites, PDF prompts, or ‘update required’ pop-ups—forward anything like this to IT immediately.”
