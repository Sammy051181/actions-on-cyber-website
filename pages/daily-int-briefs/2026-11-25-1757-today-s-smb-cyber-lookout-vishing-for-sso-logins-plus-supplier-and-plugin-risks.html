Daily SMB Cyber Intelligence Brief

Today’s SMB cyber lookout: vishing for SSO logins, plus supplier and plugin risks

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Wednesday 25 November 2026, 17:57 UK time
Today’s look-out: Phone-call (vishing) takeover of cloud/SSO accounts leading to data theft and extortion

What to look out for today

Phone-based scams (vishing) are being used to trick staff into handing over single sign-on (SSO) or cloud account access, leading to data theft and extortion. This is being reported as affecting organisations including those in the UK, and including sectors many SMEs rely on (legal, finance, healthcare and professional services).

In parallel, there’s a supplier / platform risk angle today: an education-sector SaaS provider (Turnitin) has disclosed unauthorised activity on certain customer-facing systems, and WordPress has blocked a set of plugins after a suspected developer account compromise (a reminder that plugin supply chains can be targeted).

Why this matters to smaller businesses

  • Vishing bypasses email filters and can reach finance, reception, HR and senior staff directly.
  • If attackers get SSO access, they can often reach email, files, CRM, accounting, and other connected apps in one hit.
  • Extortion attempts don’t require ransomware—stolen data + pressure tactics can still cause downtime, reputational damage and regulatory headaches.
  • Supplier incidents and plugin compromises can create follow-on scams (fake support calls, “reset your password” prompts, fake invoices) even when your own systems weren’t hacked.

Warning signs

  • An unexpected call claiming to be from IT support, a cloud provider, an M365/Google partner, or a security team, asking you to “confirm” details.
  • Pressure to act urgently: “your account is being attacked”, “fraud detected”, “we must reset your SSO now”.
  • Requests to share one-time passcodes (OTP), approve MFA prompts, reset passwords during the call, or install a “support tool”.
  • Calls targeted at admins or anyone who might have elevated access (Office manager, Finance, outsourced IT contact, website admin).
  • Unusual login alerts, MFA prompts you didn’t trigger, new inbox rules, or unexpected changes to security settings.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • SSO takeover: attackers trick a user into approving an MFA prompt or revealing a code, then log in and pivot into email, files and connected SaaS.
  • Data theft then extortion: attackers grab sensitive documents or mailboxes and demand payment to avoid leaking them.
  • Supplier-incident “piggybacking”: criminals reference widely reported incidents to sound legitimate (e.g., “we’re calling about your Turnitin account” / “we detected activity, we need you to reset now”).
  • Website/plugin angle: compromised plugin supply chains can be used to gain a foothold on websites, inject malicious code, or create admin access—often via legitimate update channels or trusted developer accounts.

What to do today

  • Brief staff (5 minutes): no one should ever read out MFA codes, approve unexpected prompts, or install software because of an inbound call.
  • Adopt a “call-back” rule: hang up and call back using a known number from your contract, invoice, or official portal—not a number given by the caller.
  • Protect SSO accounts: ensure MFA is enabled for all users (especially admins) and review who has admin privileges.
  • Check for compromise clues: review recent sign-ins and security alerts for your email/SSO provider; look for new forwarding rules or unexpected mailbox delegation.
  • Website owners: confirm who manages WordPress/plugins, and ensure you can rapidly disable/remove plugins if a supplier alert lands.
  • Education customers (Turnitin): watch for suspicious emails/calls that reference the incident, and confirm any support outreach via your known account route.

Ask your IT provider

  • Do we have a documented process for handling inbound “support/security” calls (including verification and call-back)?
  • Which system is our SSO identity provider, and do we enforce MFA for everyone (especially admins)?
  • How quickly can we disable a compromised account and revoke sessions across cloud apps?
  • Do we have sign-in alerting turned on (impossible travel, suspicious MFA prompts, new devices)? Who receives those alerts?
  • For websites: who owns plugin updates, and do we have an inventory of plugins plus a rollback/disable plan?
  • What’s our plan if we receive an extortion email claiming they stole data from our cloud accounts?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

If you run commercial refrigeration/HVAC/lighting controllers (common in food retail and hospitality), ask your facilities or IT supplier whether you use Danfoss AK-SM series controllers and what the mitigation plan is—treat this as an operational resilience issue because control-system problems can quickly become business disruption.

One action today

Send a short internal note today: “We will not share MFA codes or approve login prompts during inbound calls—hang up and call back using a known number.”
