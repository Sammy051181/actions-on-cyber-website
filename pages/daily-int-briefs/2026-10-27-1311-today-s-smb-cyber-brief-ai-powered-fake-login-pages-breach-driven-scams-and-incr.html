Daily SMB Cyber Intelligence Brief

Today’s SMB cyber brief: AI-powered fake login pages, breach-driven scams, and increased account takeover risk

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Tuesday 27 October 2026, 13:11 UK time
Today’s look-out: AI-assisted phishing and account takeovers (plus breach follow-on scams)

What to look out for today

Expect a rise in convincing fake login pages designed to steal Microsoft 365/Google/HR/payroll/CRM logins and then take over accounts. Generative AI is making these pages faster to create, more believable, and more targeted at specific staff and organisations.

Also watch for follow-on scam emails that use real breach news (e.g., from large organisations and their marketing/supplier platforms) to sound credible and push staff into clicking links, sharing codes, or changing details.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

  • Email and cloud account takeover often leads to invoice fraud, payroll diversion, and “send me the code” social engineering.
  • Once an attacker gets into one inbox, they can use existing email threads to make requests look legitimate.
  • Third-party breaches (e.g., through marketing platforms used by partners) can leak enough customer details to make phishing feel “personal” and believable.

Warning signs

  • Unexpected “sign-in” or “password expired” emails/messages urging urgent action.
  • Login pages that look perfect but arrive via a link in email, SMS, Teams/Slack, or a QR code.
  • Requests to share a one-time passcode, approve an MFA prompt, or re-enter MFA codes on a webpage.
  • Suppliers/customers contacting you about strange emails “from your team”, odd payment change requests, or new bank details.
  • Unusual mailbox behaviour: new forwarding rules, missing emails, or sent items you don’t recognise.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • AI-generated lookalike login portals impersonating Microsoft/Google/payroll/IT helpdesk brands to harvest passwords and MFA codes.
  • Session hijacking / token theft style takeovers where attackers aim to bypass login friction after a user has authenticated.
  • Breach-themed lures: scammers reference real incidents (such as a large energy provider breach via a marketing platform, or large education-sector exposures) to pressure staff into “confirming” details or logging in to a fake portal.
  • Ransomware and disruption pathways: compromised accounts can be used to spread malware internally, access shared files, or reset other passwords.

What to do today

  • Tell staff: don’t log in via links in messages. Use bookmarks or type the address directly.
  • Make MFA safer: where possible, use number-matching or phishing-resistant methods and reduce reliance on SMS codes.
  • Quick admin check: review email forwarding rules and mailbox delegations for key roles (finance, payroll, directors).
  • Finance control: enforce call-back verification for any bank detail or payment instruction change (using a known number, not the email signature).
  • Prepare for breach follow-ons: if you supply services to the public, remind front-desk/customer teams that scammers may cite well-known breach stories to gain trust.

Ask your IT provider

  • Can you show us our current anti-phishing controls for Microsoft 365/Google (link scanning, domain impersonation protection, mailbox rule alerts)?
  • Do we have conditional access (e.g., blocking risky sign-ins, new countries, impossible travel) and is it monitored?
  • How quickly would we detect and respond to an account takeover (new inbox rules, mass downloads, unusual OAuth/app consents)?
  • Are high-risk users (finance, payroll, IT admins) protected with stronger MFA and tighter access controls?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

US agencies are warning that the so-called PHP2Shell issues are being exploited in attacks. For SMEs this is a reminder to inventory any internet-facing websites/apps (including those hosted by a third party) and confirm who is responsible for timely updates and monitoring—especially if your site includes contact forms, portals, or custom integrations.

One action today

Send a 2-line staff alert: “Do not sign in from links in emails/texts—use bookmarks. Never share MFA codes or approve unexpected login prompts; report them immediately.”
