Daily SMB Cyber Intelligence Brief

Today’s SMB cyber lookout: “ClickFix” web lures, Microsoft 365 weak passwords, and fast-moving WordPress attacks

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Thursday 24 September 2026, 13:43 UK time
Today’s look-out: Web-based social engineering and SaaS account takeovers leading to ransomware and fraud

What to look out for today

  • “ClickFix” style web prompts that claim your browser/device needs a quick fix (often on otherwise trusted-looking websites) and push you to follow steps to “repair”, “verify”, or “update”.
  • Microsoft 365 account compromise attempts focusing on weak/default passwords (and likely followed by mailbox access and internal phishing from real accounts).
  • WordPress sites being targeted quickly after a new security issue was disclosed—risk is highest for SMEs running their own WordPress or using small web agencies that are slow to update.
  • Ransomware operators targeting exposed IT/build tooling (e.g., systems used to manage builds or infrastructure), which can lead to business-wide disruption if an internal tool is accessible from the internet.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

These themes don’t rely on sophisticated “Hollywood hacking”. They succeed because they target normal working habits: clicking a prompt on a website, reusing a password, trusting an email from a colleague, or leaving a supplier-managed system exposed. The impact for SMEs is typically lost access to email/files, fraudulent payment requests, and service downtime while IT cleans up.

Warning signs

  • Unexpected pop-ups or pages telling staff to copy/paste steps, “run a fix”, or “complete verification” to continue (especially if it appears after searching for software, invoices, banking, delivery, or streaming).
  • Microsoft 365 alerts: impossible travel, sign-ins from unusual locations, repeated failed logins, or new forwarding rules created in mailboxes.
  • Website symptoms: WordPress admin accounts behaving oddly, new admin users appearing, new plugins/themes you didn’t approve, or site defacement/redirects.
  • IT tool symptoms: build/management portals showing unknown logins, new users/tokens, unexpected configuration changes, or abnormal outbound traffic.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • ClickFix lures: attackers use trusted-looking pages and prompts to trick staff into taking actions that lead to malware installation or credential capture—no attachment required.
  • M365 weak/default password takeovers: once inside a mailbox, attackers can read invoices, impersonate staff, set up auto-forwarding, and run “payment diversion” scams.
  • WordPress compromise: a breached site can be used to steal customer data, plant redirects/malvertising, send spam, or act as a launchpad to other systems.
  • Ransomware follow-on: access to an internal management system can be leveraged to disrupt operations, deploy tooling across the network, or exfiltrate data for extortion.

What to do today

  • Send a 2-minute staff warning: “If a website asks you to ‘fix’ or ‘verify’ by following steps, stop and report it. Don’t copy/paste commands, install ‘updates’, or enter Microsoft 365 passwords from a pop-up.”
  • Check Microsoft 365 basics: confirm MFA is on for all users, block legacy authentication where possible, and look for mailbox forwarding rules and unusual sign-ins.
  • Website owner check-in: if you run WordPress, confirm who is responsible for updates and monitoring, and ensure you can quickly disable admin access and restore from backup.
  • Review exposure of admin/management portals: ensure internal tools are not publicly reachable unless necessary, and that admin access is locked down (MFA + IP restrictions where feasible).

Ask your IT provider

  • “Can you show me which users don’t have MFA on Microsoft 365 and fix it today?”
  • “Do we have any default/shared passwords left anywhere (email, website admin, Wi‑Fi, line-of-business apps)?”
  • “Are any management consoles or build/admin tools exposed to the internet? If yes, why—and what extra controls are in place?”
  • “For our WordPress site: who patches it, how quickly, and do we have a tested restore if it’s compromised?”

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

If your business runs a WordPress website, treat updates as urgent right now: reports indicate attackers moved quickly after a newly disclosed WordPress issue. If you outsource your website, get written confirmation today on what has been updated and what monitoring/rollback is in place.

One action today

Send a short internal message today telling staff to report any “ClickFix/verification/fix your browser” prompts and to never follow on-screen “fix steps” from a website—then have IT review Microsoft 365 sign-in alerts and mailbox forwarding rules.
