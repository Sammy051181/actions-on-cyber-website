Daily SMB Cyber Intelligence Brief

Today’s SME cyber lookout: ransomware “helpers”, supplier platform data theft, and risky AI-assistant links

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Wednesday 19 August 2026, 08:53 UK time
Today’s look-out: Extortion follow‑ons and link-based data grabs (ransomware + connected apps)

What to look out for today

Keep an eye out for three themes that can hit smaller organisations quickly:

  • Ransomware “recovery helpers” emailing you and offering (for a large fee) to delete stolen data or “fix” the situation.
  • Supplier/platform data theft where attackers focus on specialist business systems (e.g., engineering/PLM platforms) to steal files rather than immediately encrypt devices.
  • Crafted links targeting AI assistants and connected apps, aiming to quietly pull data from the user’s connected services after a click.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

  • Ransomware incidents often come with follow-on scams: once a company is known to be impacted, criminals and opportunists may pile in with offers to “help”, adding cost and confusion at a bad time.
  • Specialist SaaS and supplier platforms are high-value because they contain designs, contracts, customer details and other sensitive documents that can be used for extortion.
  • AI assistant tools can widen the blast radius: if staff connect work apps (email, storage, calendars, documents), one bad click can expose more than a single inbox.

Warning signs

  • Unexpected emails claiming they can remove stolen data, “talk to the ransomware gang”, or “prove” they accessed a criminal server—especially with urgent payment demands (e.g., tens of thousands).
  • Unusual logins or downloads in specialist platforms (file repositories, product data, document vaults) outside normal hours or from unfamiliar locations.
  • Staff report odd links related to AI tools (e.g., prompts to “review this Copilot/assistant item”, “open this shared assistant link”, or “click to view connected results”).
  • Sudden requests to connect more apps to an AI assistant or to re-authorise access “to fix syncing”.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • Extortion middlemen scams: criminals contact victims during/after an incident to extract additional payments, misdirect negotiations, or collect more information.
  • Data theft without loud disruption: attackers may focus on quietly stealing sensitive files from business platforms (including supplier-hosted systems) to fuel blackmail and pressure.
  • Link-led data access: a crafted link can be used to trick a user into triggering data access from connected apps within an AI assistant session, turning “one click” into broader data exposure.

What to do today

  • Brief staff (2 minutes): do not engage with unsolicited “ransomware recovery” emails; forward to IT/security and management immediately.
  • Lock down AI assistant connections: review which work apps are connected, remove anything non-essential, and limit who is allowed to connect business systems.
  • Check audit logs for key platforms that store valuable documents (cloud storage, document management, specialist SaaS): look for unusual bulk downloads, new API tokens, or new admin accounts.
  • Rehearse your incident comms: confirm who decides on extortion demands, who talks to insurers, and who communicates with customers/suppliers.

Ask your IT provider

  • Do we have an agreed process for handling ransomware/extortion inbound emails (triage, preservation of evidence, who responds)?
  • Which of our business-critical SaaS platforms are monitored for unusual downloads/admin changes, and how quickly would we be alerted?
  • What controls are in place for AI assistant tools and connected apps (least-privilege access, approvals, logging, and removal of stale connections)?
  • If a supplier platform is compromised, what’s our containment plan (account resets, token revocation, customer notification support)?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

If you run specialist self-hosted platforms (including AI/automation tooling) ask your IT provider whether any of your internet-facing services are seeing active scanning/exploitation attempts and whether monitoring/updates are being prioritised for those systems. Focus on reducing exposure and improving detection rather than rushing changes without a plan.

One action today

Send a short internal note today: “Do not respond to any ‘ransomware recovery’ or ‘data deletion for a fee’ emails—forward to IT/management immediately,” and ensure your helpdesk knows the escalation route.
