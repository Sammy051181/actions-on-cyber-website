Today’s SME Cyber Brief: vCenter attacks, Windows 11 auto-upgrades, and ransomware “recovery” scams

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Saturday 21 November 2026, 15:20 UK time
Today’s look-out: Business disruption + supplier/IT estate risk + ransomware response scams

What to look out for today

  • VMware vCenter being actively attacked: security updates have been released for a vCenter Server authentication-bypass issue reportedly exploited since September. If your IT provider hosts or manages vCenter, this is a priority risk.
  • Windows 11 auto-upgrades on unmanaged devices: Microsoft has begun automatically upgrading eligible PCs that aren’t managed by an IT department. This can trigger unexpected downtime, app compatibility issues, and support tickets—especially for small firms with “bring your own” or lightly managed laptops.
  • Ransomware “recovery” service scams: a ransomware-help firm’s CTO has been indicted after authorities alleged the company simply paid attackers for a decryptor and charged victims a markup while claiming advanced recovery capabilities.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

SMEs often rely on a small number of critical systems and suppliers (hosted infrastructure, outsourced IT, or a single server admin). A vCenter compromise can affect multiple servers at once, creating major disruption.

Unplanned OS upgrades can interrupt trading, payroll/finance work, printing, line-of-business apps, and remote access—particularly on machines not centrally managed.

After a ransomware incident, pressure and urgency make it easier to be overcharged or misled. Paying criminals (directly or indirectly) can also create repeat-targeting risk and doesn’t guarantee full recovery.

Warning signs

  • Sudden inability to access multiple internal systems/servers at once (especially virtualised workloads).
  • Unexpected admin account activity or access prompts relating to virtualisation management tools.
  • Staff reporting their PC “upgraded itself” overnight, new login prompts, missing apps, broken printers, or slow performance.
  • A third-party “ransomware recovery” firm that won’t explain their method, refuses to put steps in writing, or pushes for urgent payment/retainer before basic triage.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • Targeting exposed management systems: attackers focus on high-impact admin consoles (like virtualisation management) because one foothold can cascade across many servers.
  • Blending into change noise: if lots of endpoints are auto-upgrading, attackers may hide malicious activity among normal “system update” disruption and helpdesk churn.
  • Post-incident pressure tactics: scammers and unethical brokers exploit urgency after ransomware by claiming specialist decryption capabilities, while actually just acting as a paid middleman.

What to do today

  • Confirm whether you use VMware vCenter (directly or via your IT provider/host). If yes, ask for written confirmation of their mitigation/update plan and whether they have seen any suspicious activity.
  • Inventory “unmanaged” Windows PCs (home/owner laptops used for business, spare reception PCs, volunteer devices, etc.). Decide who is allowed to use them for email, banking, and admin portals.
  • Set expectations for Windows 11 upgrades: warn staff that some PCs may auto-upgrade and to report issues early (don’t “work around” by disabling security tools).
  • Prepare a ransomware response rule: if hit, you will (1) contact your IT provider/insurer first, (2) preserve evidence, (3) validate any third-party “recovery” firm with references and a clear statement of method and costs.

Ask your IT provider

  • Do we have vCenter anywhere in our estate (including hosted environments), and is it internet-exposed in any way?
  • Have you applied the vendor updates/mitigations for the vCenter issue being exploited, and when was that completed?
  • What monitoring is in place for unusual admin logins/changes in the virtualisation environment?
  • Which of our endpoints are unmanaged, and how are updates, disk encryption, endpoint protection and admin rights handled on those devices?
  • If we suffer ransomware, what is our approved process for engaging external incident response or “recovery” services, and who signs off costs?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

If you run VMware vCenter (even indirectly via a hosting/IT provider), treat the newly released vCenter security updates as urgent because the issue is reported as exploited in the wild. Also expect some Windows 11 systems to change automatically if they are not centrally managed—plan for support impact and check that security tools remain enabled after any upgrade.

One action today

Email (or message) your IT provider today: confirm in writing whether you have any VMware vCenter exposure and the date/time they completed the recommended security update/mitigation, plus what they’re doing to monitor for suspicious admin activity.
