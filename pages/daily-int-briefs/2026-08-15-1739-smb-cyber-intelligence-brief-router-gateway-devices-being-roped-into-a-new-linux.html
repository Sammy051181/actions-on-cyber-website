SMB Cyber Intelligence Brief: Router/gateway devices being roped into a new Linux botnet

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

Moderate Saturday 15 August 2026, 17:39 UK time
Today’s look-out: Router and gateway compromise turning devices into traffic relays

What to look out for today

A newly reported Linux botnet (Mirai-based and modular) is targeting internet-facing gateway devices (such as routers) and turning them into SOCKS5 traffic relay nodes. In plain terms: attackers take over vulnerable or poorly secured edge devices and use them as “middlemen” to route other people’s malicious traffic.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

  • Business disruption risk: a compromised router can cause slow or unstable internet, broken VoIP/video calls, and intermittent access to cloud services.
  • Security and reputation risk: your connection could be used to relay suspicious activity, which can trigger ISP warnings, IP reputation blocks, or downstream service lockouts.
  • Hidden impact: router compromise often doesn’t look like a “normal virus” on a PC, so it can persist unnoticed.

Warning signs

  • Unexpected internet slowdowns, especially outside office hours.
  • Users reporting random dropouts on Teams/Zoom/VoIP calls.
  • Unexplained spikes in outbound traffic (upload) on your broadband line.
  • Firewall/router admin account changes you can’t explain (new users, changed passwords, altered remote management settings).
  • Customers, suppliers, or SaaS tools flagging your public IP for suspicious activity.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • Taking over internet-facing gateway devices and converting them into traffic relays (SOCKS5), helping criminals hide where their activity comes from.
  • Using router control to observe or interfere with business traffic patterns (even if they can’t read encrypted content, they can still disrupt).
  • Leveraging compromised edge devices as a stepping stone to further attempts against PCs, servers, or cloud accounts.

What to do today

  • Inventory your edge devices: list the make/model of your router/firewall, who manages it, and where it’s hosted (office, data centre, ISP-managed).
  • Check exposure: confirm remote administration is not openly available on the internet unless there is a strong business reason and it’s tightly controlled.
  • Change admin credentials (especially if they’re shared across sites or have been in place for years) and ensure MFA is enabled where the platform supports it.
  • Review outbound traffic: ask your ISP/MSP for a quick check of unusual outbound connections or traffic spikes.
  • Have an escalation plan: if you suspect router compromise, know who can isolate/replace the device quickly to restore service.

Ask your IT provider

  • Which of our routers/firewalls are internet-facing and what remote management paths exist?
  • Do we have alerting for unusual outbound traffic or changes to edge device configuration?
  • What is our process and time-to-respond if a router is suspected compromised (isolate, factory reset, reconfigure, replace)?
  • Are our branch/office devices configured to block unauthorised proxying/relay behaviour and are logs retained?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

This story is a reminder that routers and gateway devices need the same “keep it current” discipline as laptops and servers. If your router/firewall is supplier-managed, confirm the provider is applying firmware updates and reviewing internet-exposed settings; if it’s self-managed, ensure firmware updates are part of your regular maintenance routine.

One action today

Confirm today whether any office router/firewall has internet-exposed admin access, and if so, restrict it and rotate the admin password.
