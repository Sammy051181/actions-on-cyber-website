Today’s SMB cyber lookout: infostealers, MFA recovery scams, and vendor breach ripple effects

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Wednesday 09 September 2026, 20:16 UK time
Today’s look-out: Account takeover via infostealers and account-recovery social engineering (plus supplier breach ripple effects)

What to look out for today

Three patterns are worth flagging to staff and your IT support today:

  • Infostealer-driven account takeovers – attackers use stolen browser data and session tokens from infected PCs to access online tools, including AI services, sometimes in ways that can bypass MFA.
  • MFA “account recovery” as the weak link – attackers increasingly target password reset and recovery processes (helpdesks, recovery email/phone, and identity checks) rather than attacking MFA head-on.
  • Supplier/vendor incidents – a third-party vendor incident can expose customer or patient data, triggering notification, support and reputational impact for downstream organisations.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

  • AI and SaaS accounts are now business systems: if an AI account or other SaaS login is taken over, it can expose sensitive prompts, files, integrations, billing and API usage.
  • “We have MFA” is not the end of the story: if attackers can persuade someone to reset MFA or change recovery details, they can still take over accounts.
  • Third-party risk is real: even if you do everything right internally, a vendor’s incident can create mandatory comms, customer questions, and knock-on fraud attempts using breach details.

Warning signs

  • Unexpected password reset or MFA reset prompts for email, Microsoft 365/Google, accounting/payroll, CRM, AI tools or other SaaS.
  • “Support” calls or emails pushing urgency: "I need you to approve an account recovery" or "we’re re-verifying your login".
  • New or unfamiliar devices/sessions showing in account sign-in history.
  • Unusual AI tool usage or billing spikes (token/API consumption), new keys, or integrations you don’t recognise.
  • After any publicised breach: increased “follow-up” emails asking you to confirm details, re-login, or change payment instructions.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • Infostealers on a PC harvest saved passwords, cookies/sessions and tokens. Attackers then log in as the user without needing the phone prompt you expect.
  • Recovery-path social engineering: attackers pose as staff or executives to convince a helpdesk (internal or outsourced) to reset passwords, change MFA methods, or update recovery email/phone.
  • Vendor incident “piggybacking”: criminals use breach headlines to make scam messages feel credible (fake data breach notices, fake security alerts, fake account verification).

What to do today

  • Pick your top 5 business-critical accounts (email, Microsoft 365/Google, finance/banking, payroll, CRM, AI/SaaS) and review: MFA enabled, recovery email/phone correct, and recent sign-in history.
  • Lock down account recovery: require strong identity checks before any password/MFA reset (especially for senior staff and finance).
  • Reduce token/session risk: ensure staff use managed devices for business logins; avoid saving passwords in unmanaged browsers on shared PCs; keep browsers and endpoints protected.
  • Brief staff: “No one will ever pressure you to ‘approve’ an account recovery.” Use a call-back to a known internal number for any reset request.
  • Vendor breach readiness: confirm who monitors supplier incident notifications, and how you will message customers if your vendor notifies you of data exposure.

Ask your IT provider

  • How do we verify identity before you (or we) perform password resets or MFA resets—what is the written process?
  • Can you provide a weekly report of suspicious sign-ins / impossible travel / new device logins for Microsoft 365/Google and other key SaaS?
  • Do we have controls to detect or reduce infostealer impact (endpoint protection coverage, browser hardening, device compliance for logins)?
  • For AI tools we use: how are API keys/tokens stored, rotated, and monitored for unusual usage?
  • What is our supplier incident playbook—who is notified, what evidence is collected, and how do we assess downstream exposure?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

If you use managed firewall/security platforms, ask your IT provider whether any internet-facing management consoles are at risk from currently exploited issues and whether monitoring and mitigations are in place. Focus on exposure and detection rather than technical detail.

One action today

Run a 15-minute “account recovery lockdown” check today: confirm recovery email/phone details and reset procedures for your email and finance systems, and remind staff to treat any reset/MFA change request as suspicious unless verified via a known call-back route.
