Daily SMB Cyber Intelligence Brief

Today’s SME cyber look-out: trojanised extensions and persuasive “work tool” social engineering

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Sunday 15 November 2026, 14:44 UK time
Today’s look-out: Supplier/SaaS and extension marketplace risk, plus social engineering aimed at getting staff to install or approve tools

What to look out for today

Be extra cautious about new browser/IDE extensions and “AI helper” tools being installed or updated, especially if staff are prompted to install something to “fix access”, “enable Claude/AI”, “improve productivity”, or “review a document”. Recent reporting describes an incident where an official-looking coding/AI extension was compromised after a supplier access key was exposed.

Separately, reporting on a disrupted North Korean social engineering campaign reinforces a familiar risk for SMEs: well-crafted persuasion and impersonation used to get people to hand over credentials, approve access, or install software.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

  • Extensions and plugins can become a back door: if a compromised extension is installed, it may access code, passwords, sessions, or internal data depending on permissions.
  • Supplier key leaks become your problem: even if your business did nothing wrong, a vendor’s exposed key can lead to malicious updates or unauthorised access paths.
  • Charities, schools and professional services are attractive targets because email accounts, payment processes, and sensitive data can be leveraged for fraud and onward attacks.

Warning signs

  • Staff receive messages urging them to install a “required” extension/tool to continue work or regain access.
  • Pop-ups or emails claiming an extension needs an urgent re-login, new permissions, or a “quick fix”.
  • Unplanned prompts for admin approval to install developer tools, AI assistants, scripting add-ons, or remote support utilities.
  • Unusual account behaviour shortly after a new extension/tool is installed (unexpected MFA prompts, mailbox rules, new sign-ins, odd sent emails).
  • Developers or power users report an extension updating unexpectedly or behaving differently.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • Trojanised marketplace items: attackers abuse stolen supplier keys or compromised developer accounts to publish malicious versions of popular extensions.
  • Impersonation and urgency: attackers pose as internal IT, a vendor, or a colleague to push installations/approvals quickly.
  • Credential capture: “sign in to continue” flows are used to steal logins, or to trick users into approving access they don’t understand.
  • Targeting finance workflows: once an account is accessed, attackers pivot to invoice/payment diversion or payroll change attempts.

What to do today

  • Set a rule: no one installs new extensions/plugins (browser or IDE) without approval from IT (or your MSP) and a recorded business reason.
  • Quick audit: ask for a list of newly installed or recently updated extensions on company-managed devices (start with developer machines and admins).
  • Reduce permissions: remove any extensions that request broad access and are not essential for work.
  • Reinforce reporting: tell staff to forward any “install this tool/extension” messages to IT, especially if urgent or unusual.
  • Protect key accounts: ensure MFA is enabled on email and admin accounts; double-check that finance accounts have stronger controls.

Ask your IT provider

  • Do we have a documented process for approving and deploying browser/IDE extensions and other add-ons?
  • Can we see a report of extensions installed across our fleet, and alerts for new installs/updates?
  • Do we restrict who can install extensions (standard users vs admins) on managed devices?
  • How do we detect suspicious behaviour after an extension install (unusual sign-ins, token theft indicators, mailbox rule changes)?
  • Do we have an incident playbook for “suspected trojanised tool/extension” (isolation, credential reset, log review, user communications)?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

No broad SME patch emergency is indicated by today’s items. If you run self-hosted, niche internet-facing services, keep an eye on vendor/security advisories and apply routine updates promptly—but today’s priority for most SMEs is controlling extensions/add-ons and tightening approval processes.

One action today

Send a same-day staff note: “Do not install or approve any new browser/IDE extensions or ‘AI helper’ tools unless IT/MSP confirms in writing,” and route all related requests to a single inbox/ticket queue.
