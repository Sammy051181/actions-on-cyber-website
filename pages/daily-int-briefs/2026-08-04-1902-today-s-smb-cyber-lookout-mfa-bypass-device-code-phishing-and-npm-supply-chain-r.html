Today’s SMB cyber lookout: MFA-bypass “device code” phishing and npm supply‑chain risk

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Tuesday 04 August 2026, 19:02 UK time
Today’s look-out: Account takeover scams and software supply‑chain compromise

What to look out for today

Two practical risks stand out today for UK SMEs:

  • MFA-bypass phishing using “device code” sign-in flows (staff are tricked into approving a sign-in that hands attackers a valid session/token).
  • Software supply-chain issues in the npm ecosystem (malicious or compromised packages spreading widely, potentially impacting web apps, internal tools, and vendors who build or run software for you).

Why this matters to smaller businesses

  • MFA doesn’t always save you if staff are persuaded to complete a legitimate-looking sign-in approval flow. That can lead to email takeover, invoice fraud, data theft, and downstream attacks on customers/suppliers.
  • Supply-chain attacks can hit you indirectly: your website agency, outsourced devs, MSP, or a SaaS provider may depend on npm packages. A compromised dependency can cause data exposure, service disruption, or backdoor access.
  • Business impact is often operational: mailbox lockouts, password resets, payroll/payment delays, reputational harm, and time lost to incident response.

Warning signs

  • Emails/DMs telling staff to “sign in using a code on another device”, “pair your account”, or “verify your login” with a short code.
  • Staff receive unexpected sign-in prompts or messages implying “someone is trying to access your account”.
  • Sudden new inbox rules, forwarding addresses, or “sent items” the user didn’t send (common after account takeover).
  • IT/dev teams notice unexpected dependency changes, new npm packages appearing, or unusual build behaviour in CI/CD.
  • Vendors reporting “a dependency issue” or asking you to disable security controls to “get things working”.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • Device-code phishing: attackers persuade a user to complete a legitimate OAuth device authorisation flow, letting the attacker gain access without stealing the password in the traditional way.
  • Phishing-as-a-service tooling: packaged kits lower the barrier, increasing the volume of convincing, fast-moving campaigns.
  • npm supply-chain compromise: malicious packages (or mass-compromised packages) can be pulled into applications during development/build, potentially enabling data theft or remote control depending on what the package does and where it runs.

What to do today

  • Warn staff (today): never follow sign-in instructions from unsolicited emails/texts. If prompted to enter/approve a device code, stop and verify with IT via a known channel.
  • Check email account protections: ensure admin accounts use strong MFA, and review conditional access / sign-in policies where available.
  • Hunt for takeover indicators: review mailbox forwarding rules and suspicious inbox rules for finance and senior staff.
  • If you build software or use an agency: ask how they are responding to the npm supply-chain news (dependency pinning, vetted registries, monitoring for unexpected dependency changes).
  • Prepare for invoice fraud: remind finance teams to verify bank detail changes using a call-back to a known number, not email replies.

Ask your IT provider

  • Can you detect and block device-code / token-based sign-in abuse (alerting on unusual device authorisations, impossible travel, risky sign-ins)?
  • Do we have a standard playbook for suspected mailbox takeover (disable sessions, reset passwords, remove rules, check forwarding, notify contacts)?
  • Which of our suppliers (website/app developers, MSP tooling, internal apps) depend on npm, and what controls do they use to prevent compromised dependencies reaching production?
  • How quickly would you spot and respond to new email forwarding rules on finance mailboxes?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

CISA has added newly exploited issues to its catalogue, including an item affecting N-able N-central (used by some managed IT providers). If you use an MSP, ask them to confirm whether their remote monitoring/management stack is affected and what mitigations they’ve applied.

One action today

Send a same-day staff note: “If you’re asked to enter or approve a sign-in ‘device code’ (or get unexpected MFA prompts), stop and report it—do not proceed.”
