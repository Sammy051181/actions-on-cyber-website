Daily SMB Cyber Intelligence Brief

Today’s SMB cyber lookout: fake online shops stealing card details, extortion scams, and browser risk

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Wednesday 09 September 2026, 13:19 UK time
Today’s look-out: Payment-card fraud via fake online shops + scam-driven extortion and phishing

What to look out for today

  • Fake online shops stealing card details: A large fraud network (“DoppelCart”) is running huge numbers of look‑alike e‑commerce sites designed to capture payment card details.
  • Extortion and harassment scams: Sextortion/cyberstalking continues to be monetised (including use of AI-generated content), and these tactics often spill into workplaces via email and social media threats.
  • Browser risk and “click-first” attacks: Google has patched another Chrome issue reported as exploited in attacks—this increases the chance of real-world phishing or malicious adverts succeeding if devices aren’t promptly updated.
  • Hosting/supplier exposure: If you run websites or email on shared hosting, today’s hosting-platform security news is a reminder that one compromised account can become a wider service incident.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

SMEs are often targeted through payment and people rather than deep technical attacks. Fake shops can trap staff buying supplies or customers buying from your brand look‑alike site. Extortion emails can cause panic, reputational damage and urgent “pay now” decisions. Browser exploitation makes everyday activity—opening links, ads, and documents—higher risk on unpatched machines.

Warning signs

  • Staff report a “too good to be true” supplier site, unusual discounting, or a new shop domain that looks similar to a known brand.
  • Checkout pages that feel “off”: limited payment options, push for card entry only, odd language, or poor contact details/returns info.
  • Emails claiming kompromat/explicit material, threats to share content, or pressure to pay quickly (often with shame/urgency tactics).
  • Sudden spikes in browser crashes, security warnings, or unexpected sign-in prompts after clicking ads/search results.
  • Customer queries about orders you didn’t receive, or customers finding “your shop” on a different domain than your real one.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • Look‑alike shopping domains promoted via search adverts, social posts, or direct spam—capturing card details at checkout.
  • Extortion emails sent to generic addresses (accounts@, info@) or individuals, aiming to trigger quick payment or internal disruption.
  • Browser-based compromise where an already-exploited weakness increases the success rate of malicious pages and drive-by scams on unpatched devices.
  • Supplier/hosting cascade: compromise of one hosted account or weak credential could lead to email forwarding abuse, website changes, or wider service disruption.

What to do today

  • Send a 2-minute staff note: “Be cautious buying from unfamiliar online shops; don’t use business cards on new suppliers without checks; report extortion emails immediately; don’t engage.”
  • Buying controls: Require first-time suppliers to be verified (Companies House details, phone call to a known number, returns address, and independent reviews).
  • Card protection: Use virtual cards/low-limit cards for online purchasing where possible, and keep separate cards for subscriptions vs. ad-hoc purchases.
  • Brand/domain check: Search for look‑alike domains of your business name and ensure your real domain is clearly shown on invoices, email signatures and social profiles.
  • Report and preserve evidence: If an extortion email lands, keep the message, note any payment instructions, and escalate internally—don’t reply or pay.

Ask your IT provider

  • Are Chrome (and managed browsers) updating promptly across all devices, including shared/front-desk PCs?
  • Do we have web filtering/DNS protection to reduce access to newly created scam domains?
  • What controls do we have for card-data exposure (policy, logging, and endpoint protections), and who is alerted if a machine shows signs of browser compromise?
  • If our website/email is hosted, what is our blast-radius plan if the hosting environment or a single account is compromised (restores, lock-down steps, communications)?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

Google has patched another Chrome issue reported as being exploited in attacks, and Microsoft has released an unusually large batch of fixes. For SMEs, the key point is operational: ensure your IT support can rapidly update browsers and has a tested, prioritised patch routine so critical updates don’t wait for the next “quiet week.”

One action today

Send a short all-staff message today: “Avoid first-time online shops for business purchases without verification; report extortion/threat emails immediately; don’t click paid search ‘shop’ links for urgent buys.”
