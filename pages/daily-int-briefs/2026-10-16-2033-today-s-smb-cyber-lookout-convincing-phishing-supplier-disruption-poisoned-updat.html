Daily SMB Cyber Intelligence Brief

Today’s SMB cyber lookout: convincing phishing + supplier disruption + “poisoned update” supply-chain risk

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Friday 16 October 2026, 20:33 UK time
Today’s look-out: Impersonation phishing and supplier/update-chain disruption

What to look out for today

  • More convincing phishing and smishing (email and text) designed to steal logins or get staff to install malware—often made to look like real internal messages, suppliers, or “event invites”.
  • Google/Microsoft-style login lures that can capture MFA codes (so “we have MFA” isn’t a complete safety net if staff approve unexpected prompts or enter codes into fake pages).
  • Supplier/service disruption after a cyber incident—even if you’re not the target, a supplier attack can knock out systems you rely on.
  • Software update supply-chain risk: attackers tampering with a vendor’s update mechanism (briefly) so that a normal update check can pull down something malicious.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

SMEs, charities and schools are often targeted through people and suppliers rather than “hacking the firewall”. If attackers get one mailbox or one cloud account, they can:

  • send believable invoice/payment-change emails from a real account,
  • pull data from shared drives and mailboxes,
  • set up forwarding rules to watch finance conversations,
  • disrupt operations if a critical vendor or managed service provider is impacted.

Separately, supply-chain tampering of software updates is a reminder that “trusted tools” (backup, remote support, utilities) can become a route in if the vendor’s update infrastructure is compromised.

Warning signs

  • Unexpected “event invite” asking you to sign in to view an agenda, speaker pack, or “shared document”.
  • Texts (smishing) urging immediate action: “account suspended”, “urgent security check”, “invoice overdue”, “new voicemail”, “missed delivery”.
  • MFA prompts you didn’t initiate (push fatigue) or requests to read back/pass on a code.
  • Login pages that feel slightly off (odd wording, extra steps, unexpected re-auth, unusual consent screens).
  • Supplier ‘service status’ messages arriving via email asking you to log in through a link, or to “reconfirm credentials”.
  • Unplanned software updates or tools requesting elevated permissions unexpectedly.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • Impersonation and highly realistic lures: attackers craft emails/texts that closely resemble legitimate communications to capture credentials or trick staff into opening malicious content.
  • Adversary-in-the-middle (AitM) style phishing: a fake sign-in flow relays the login in real time to capture passwords and MFA codes, allowing attackers to access cloud accounts.
  • Supplier incident “follow-on scams”: criminals use news of a disruption to send fake “support”, “reconnect”, or “billing” messages to customers.
  • Update-chain compromise: if a vendor’s update servers are tampered with (even briefly), any organisation checking for updates during that window could be exposed.

What to do today

  • Tell staff one simple rule for the day: don’t sign in via links from emails/texts—open the site/app directly from a bookmark or known URL.
  • Reinforce MFA hygiene: treat unexpected MFA prompts as a red flag and report them immediately.
  • Finance check: require a call-back (using a known number, not the email signature) for any bank detail change, “urgent payment”, or new payee request.
  • Supplier dependency check: list your top 5 critical providers (IT/MSP, payroll, payments, email, line-of-business SaaS) and confirm you have a way to operate if one is down for 24–72 hours.
  • Software update caution: avoid ad-hoc installs and ensure updates for core tools are managed/approved (especially backup and remote/admin tools).

Ask your IT provider

  • Do we have phishing-resistant MFA options available (e.g., passkeys/security keys) for admin and finance accounts?
  • What controls are in place to detect or block impossible travel, suspicious logins, and mailbox forwarding rules in our email tenant?
  • How do we verify supplier incidents (status pages, known contacts, comms channels) without relying on links in emails?
  • Which third-party tools are allowed to auto-update, and do we have a process to pause/validate updates if a vendor is suspected of compromise?
  • If a key supplier is disrupted, what’s our fallback plan for invoices, payroll, customer comms, and access to critical documents?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

If you use internet-connected cameras or similar IoT systems managed via third-party apps/platforms, check with your IT provider that they are inventoried and centrally managed. IoT weaknesses are often exploited opportunistically, and SMEs commonly forget these devices exist until there’s an incident.

One action today

Send a same-day staff note: “No logins from email/text links; if you get an unexpected MFA prompt or ‘event invite’ sign-in request, report it immediately.”
