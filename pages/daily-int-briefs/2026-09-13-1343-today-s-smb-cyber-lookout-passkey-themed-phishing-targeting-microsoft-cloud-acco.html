Daily SMB Cyber Intelligence Brief

Today’s SMB cyber lookout: passkey-themed phishing targeting Microsoft cloud accounts

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Sunday 13 September 2026, 13:43 UK time
Today’s look-out: Cloud account takeover + CEO/finance impersonation scams

What to look out for today

Watch for a wave of passkey-themed phishing and high-volume “CEO/finance” scam emails designed to trick staff into handing over access to Microsoft cloud accounts and enabling data theft.

  • Emails that claim your organisation must “upgrade to passkeys”, “re-verify sign-in”, or “avoid account suspension”.
  • Messages impersonating senior leaders (e.g. CEO) pushing urgent payments, refunds, or “finance checks”.
  • Unusual sign-in prompts or MFA/passkey requests that arrive out of the blue.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

Most SMEs depend on Microsoft 365 for email, files and collaboration. If an attacker gets into a cloud account, they can:

  • Read email and files, harvest contacts, and steal sensitive documents.
  • Send convincing “from your account” payment or invoice fraud emails to customers and suppliers.
  • Set up forwarding rules to quietly monitor communications and payment details.
  • Create disruption (lockouts, account changes) that stops work and harms trust.

Warning signs

  • Unexpected passkey/MFA prompts or sign-in approvals you didn’t initiate.
  • Emails with urgency and authority: “Do this now”, “confidential”, “I’m in a meeting—just pay it”.
  • Links that go to unfamiliar domains or a “Microsoft-looking” page that doesn’t match your normal sign-in flow.
  • Sudden changes to supplier bank details, or payment requests that bypass normal approvals.
  • New inbox rules, auto-forwarding, or “sent items” you don’t recognise.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • Social engineering: convincing staff to follow a fake “passkey setup” or “security upgrade” journey.
  • Impersonation at scale: mass-mailing scams that look like they came from executives or finance leaders.
  • Cloud foothold to fraud: once inside Microsoft accounts, using real email threads to launch invoice/payment diversion scams.
  • Data exfiltration: accessing files and mailboxes to collect commercially sensitive information.

What to do today

  • Brief staff (especially finance/admin): no payments or bank-detail changes via email alone—use a known phone number or established callback process.
  • Reinforce “deny by default” for prompts: if you didn’t start a login, do not approve any passkey/MFA request—report it.
  • Check email rules: look for new forwarding rules or suspicious inbox rules in key mailboxes (finance, directors, shared inboxes).
  • Tighten access: ensure multi-factor authentication is enforced for cloud accounts and that high-risk sign-ins are monitored.
  • Prepare a response: have a simple internal playbook for “possible Microsoft account compromise” (who to call, how to lock accounts, how to warn customers/suppliers).

Ask your IT provider

  • Are we monitoring for unusual Microsoft 365 sign-ins (new locations, impossible travel, atypical devices)?
  • Do we alert on new mailbox forwarding rules and suspicious inbox rules—especially for finance and leadership accounts?
  • Can we enforce strong authentication for all users and block risky legacy sign-in methods?
  • Do we have an agreed rapid process to lock an account, revoke sessions, and investigate if a user reports a suspicious prompt?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

Not a patch-led issue today. The main risk is social engineering and cloud account takeover, so focus on staff reporting, payment verification controls, and Microsoft 365 sign-in/rule monitoring.

One action today

Send a same-day warning to all staff: “If you didn’t initiate a sign-in, do not approve any passkey/MFA prompt—report it immediately; and never change payment details from an email request without a trusted callback.”
