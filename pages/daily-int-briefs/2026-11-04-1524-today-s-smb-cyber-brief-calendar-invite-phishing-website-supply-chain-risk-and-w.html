Daily SMB Cyber Intelligence Brief

Today’s SMB cyber brief: Calendar invite phishing, website supply‑chain risk, and WordPress attacks in the wild

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Wednesday 04 November 2026, 15:24 UK time
Today’s look-out: Phishing that bypasses MFA + website/supplier compromise leading to account takeover

What to look out for today

  • Google Calendar invite phishing that tricks staff into clicking through to fake sign-in pages and can hijack Google sessions (even where MFA is enabled).
  • “Watering hole” / website supply-chain attacks where a third-party web analytics script is tampered with, putting visitors (including staff on mobiles) at risk.
  • WordPress sites being attacked “in the wild” via a recently patched flaw—risking site takeover, malware injection, or redirecting customers to scams.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

  • Account takeover can become payment fraud quickly: if an attacker gets into email/calendar, they can read threads, set forwarding rules, and impersonate staff for invoice/payment-change scams.
  • You may be affected even if you’re not the target: if you (or your web agency) use third-party scripts/analytics, a supplier compromise can expose your staff and customers just by visiting your site.
  • Website disruption hits revenue and trust: a compromised WordPress site can be defaced, used to harvest customer details, or silently redirect users—often noticed late.

Warning signs

  • Unexpected calendar invites with links to “view document”, “join meeting”, “security alert”, or “account verification”.
  • Sign-in pages that look right but appear after multiple redirects, or request you to re-authenticate when you’ve recently signed in.
  • Staff reporting they are logged out of Google/Microsoft services unexpectedly, or seeing unfamiliar “new login” alerts they didn’t trigger.
  • Your website starts redirecting users, shows pop-ups, runs slowly, or browsers warn it’s unsafe.
  • Customers/suppliers report strange links coming from your domain, or emails “from you” that you didn’t send.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • Session hijacking beats MFA: rather than stealing a password, attackers aim to steal an already-authenticated session so they can act as the user.
  • Trusted-channel abuse: calendar invites feel “official” and get through busy inboxes; staff click faster because it looks like normal workflow.
  • Supply-chain web scripts: attackers tamper with third-party analytics/marketing code so any website using it can deliver malicious content to visitors.
  • Compromised CMS (e.g., WordPress): attackers target known, widely used site software to gain control and then add redirects, credential-harvesting pages, or further malware.

What to do today

  • Warn staff: treat unexpected calendar invites like email links—verify the sender and context before clicking.
  • Harden Google Workspace/M365 basics: review rules for external invites, link-scanning/safe browsing, and ensure users can report suspicious invites easily.
  • Check for suspicious account changes: look for new email forwarding rules, new OAuth/app consents, unusual sign-in locations, and unexplained mailbox/calendar access.
  • Website quick health check: confirm your homepage and key pages aren’t redirecting; review recent admin logins and plugin/theme changes.
  • Supplier sanity check: ask your web agency/IT provider what third-party scripts run on your site and how they monitor for tampering.

Ask your IT provider

  • Do we have alerting for suspicious Google/M365 sign-ins and signs of session hijacking (impossible travel, unusual device/browser, repeated token events)?
  • Can we restrict external calendar invites or clearly label them, and do we have an easy process for staff to report them?
  • Are we monitoring for new mailbox forwarding rules and unusual OAuth/app consents?
  • For our website: what’s our process to detect unexpected file changes, new admin users, or injected scripts?
  • Which third-party scripts/analytics tags do we run, and can we reduce them or add controls to limit impact if one is compromised?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

If your business runs WordPress (including marketing sites managed by an agency), treat this week as a priority: there is a recently patched WordPress issue reported as being actively exploited. Ask your site maintainer to confirm updates have been applied and to validate the site for unexpected changes after patching.

One action today

Send a same-day staff note: “Unexpected calendar invites are treated like phishing—don’t click links, verify the sender/context first, and report anything suspicious.”
