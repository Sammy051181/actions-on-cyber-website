Daily SMB Cyber Intelligence Brief

Today’s SME cyber lookout: AI agents with “too much access”, plus ongoing mass exploit attempts

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Saturday 24 October 2026, 16:55 UK time
Today’s look-out: AI agent misuse + mass internet exploit attempts + abandoned software risk

What to look out for today

1) “AI agents” and automation tools operating inside businesses without clear ownership (including tools that can read inboxes, access files, run actions in SaaS systems, or hold API keys and passwords). Several reports highlight that agents can be manipulated via normal business inputs (emails, websites, plugins) and may operate with broad permissions.

2) Increased background noise from mass exploit attempts against internet-facing services, with attackers attempting to enrol vulnerable systems into botnets (disruption risk, not just data theft).

3) Abandoned or unofficial software on staff devices with risky update mechanisms (a common route for malware and remote control).

Why this matters to smaller businesses

  • AI tools can become “shadow admins”: if an agent has access to mail, documents, CRM, finance platforms, or cloud storage, a single mistake or manipulation can lead to data exposure, fraudulent payments, or deletion of files.
  • SMEs are disproportionately exposed to “set and forget” automation: tools get trialled for productivity, then remain in place without review, logging, or limits.
  • Mass scanning doesn’t target by size: if you have an internet-facing service, attackers’ automated attempts can hit you the same as a large firm—leading to outages, crypto-mining, or being used as a stepping-stone.

Warning signs

  • Unexpected “automation” or “agent” behaviour: emails sent you didn’t draft, files moved/deleted, new rules in mailboxes, unusual calendar invites, or unexplained changes in SaaS records.
  • New integrations or API keys created without a change request/ticket, especially with broad scopes (e.g., full mailbox, full drive, admin-level SaaS permissions).
  • Unusual login prompts/consent screens asking staff to approve access for an app they don’t recognise.
  • Devices running abandoned/unapproved apps (often installed without IT involvement), especially where staff can’t explain why it’s needed for work.
  • Spikes in server load/bandwidth, unusual outbound connections, or repeated authentication errors on public-facing services.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • Manipulating agents through everyday content: an email, web page, or plugin content can trick an agent into taking actions using its legitimate permissions (e.g., searching documents, forwarding data, or changing settings).
  • Abusing over-permissioned integrations: once an attacker gets access to a token/API key (or tricks a user into granting access), they may not need a password to your main account.
  • Opportunistic exploitation of exposed services: automated bots probe for weaknesses in public-facing systems to drop malware, enrol devices into botnets, or create persistent access.
  • Supply-chain style risk in tooling: SDK or integration defaults (e.g., missing spending limits) can create unexpected financial or operational impact if used in payment-like workflows.

What to do today

  • Do a quick “AI agent/integration inventory”: list any agents/automation tools in use (including email assistants, CRM automations, finance workflow automations, RPA tools) and who owns them.
  • Reduce permissions: ensure agents/integrations have the minimum access needed (avoid full mailbox/drive/admin unless absolutely required), and set expiry/rotation for API keys.
  • Turn on logging and alerts for: new OAuth app consents, new API keys, mailbox rule creation, mass downloads, and unusual admin actions.
  • Reconfirm software policy on endpoints: remove abandoned/unapproved apps, and ensure updates come from trusted channels only.
  • Check your internet-facing services: confirm you know what is exposed, who monitors it, and what your plan is if exploitation causes disruption (restore steps, isolation, provider escalation).

Ask your IT provider

  • Which AI/automation tools are connected to our email, file storage and business apps, and who is the named owner for each?
  • Do we have controls to restrict AI/agents (and other integrations) from taking risky actions (e.g., sending emails externally, deleting files, changing finance/payee details)?
  • What alerts do we have for new OAuth app consents, new API keys, and suspicious mailbox rules?
  • Do we have an endpoint policy to detect/remove abandoned or unapproved apps, and can staff install software without approval?
  • What monitoring is in place for unusual traffic or scanning on our public services, and what is our containment playbook if we’re hit?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

There are reports of widespread exploit attempts against newly disclosed weaknesses in internet-facing PHP services, and separate reports of Linux file-sharing server flaws being publicly discussed before fixes. For SMEs, the key action is operational: confirm your external-facing services are inventoried, monitored, and patched via your normal change process—and that anything exposed unnecessarily is removed or locked down.

One action today

Identify every AI agent/automation/integration connected to email and file storage, name an owner for each, and immediately remove or reduce any that have broad permissions without a clear business need.
