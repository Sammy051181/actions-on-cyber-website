Today’s SMB cyber lookout: Microsoft 365 disruption + remote support tool abuse + supplier breach knock-on

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Thursday 12 November 2026, 22:14 UK time
Today’s look-out: Cloud disruption and IT supplier impersonation scams (Microsoft 365 / remote support tools)

What to look out for today

  • Microsoft 365 outage disruption (Outlook, Teams, Copilot) causing real-world business interruption and confusion.
  • Remote support tool risk (ScreenConnect): campaigns targeting ScreenConnect deployments can turn legitimate support tooling into a path to wider compromise.
  • Supplier breach ripple effects: professional services and their providers remain a route for attackers to obtain personal and business data that can be reused in convincing scams.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

When widely used services go down (like Microsoft 365), staff look for workarounds under pressure. That’s when phishing, fake “support” calls, and bogus “service restoration” emails can land. At the same time, remote support tools used by IT providers (or in-house) are high-value targets: if attackers get in, they can move quickly across multiple machines. Finally, supplier breaches can provide enough personal or account context to make invoice fraud and HR/payroll scams more believable.

Warning signs

  • Emails or Teams messages claiming to be “Microsoft Support” or “Microsoft 365 Admin” asking you to re-enter your password, approve a login, or install a tool “to fix the outage”.
  • Unexpected contact from “IT support” urging you to allow a remote session urgently, especially if you weren’t expecting it.
  • Unusual remote access prompts on PCs/servers, or staff reporting “someone moved my mouse”.
  • New or changed payment details arriving during disruption (“we can’t access our usual email right now, please pay this new account”).
  • Customers/staff receiving targeted messages that reference dependent details or HR-style personal info (a common outcome after third-party incidents).

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • Outage-themed phishing: attackers use real disruption as cover for fake password resets, MFA fatigue prompts, or “temporary login portals”.
  • IT/helpdesk impersonation: phone calls to reception/finance saying, “Microsoft/your IT provider is fixing an incident, we need access now.”
  • Abuse of remote support infrastructure: where ScreenConnect (or similar) is exposed or poorly controlled, it can be used to gain an interactive foothold and pivot further.
  • Supplier-breach-enabled social engineering: personal or organisational details gleaned from third-party breaches are used to craft more convincing invoice, payroll, or account takeover attempts.

What to do today

  • Set a “disruption rule” for staff today: during service issues, nobody should approve logins, MFA prompts, or install software from links in messages claiming to fix the problem.
  • Use known-good channels: if Microsoft 365 is flaky, use your pre-agreed alternative (phone tree, SMS, secondary email domain) rather than ad-hoc personal email.
  • Lock down remote support: confirm who can start sessions, require strong admin controls, and ensure session alerts are enabled and reviewed.
  • Finance controls: re-brief the team that payment detail changes must be verified by calling a known number (from your supplier master data, not an email signature).
  • Prepare a short customer comms line in case email/Teams is disrupted (what’s impacted, what isn’t, and how to reach you).

Ask your IT provider

  • Are we (or any of your tools) using ScreenConnect? If yes, how is it exposed to the internet, and what extra controls are in place (MFA, IP allow-listing, session approvals, logging)?
  • How will you notify us if you need remote access today, and what is the phrase/process staff should use to verify it’s really you?
  • Do we have a tested business continuity plan for Microsoft 365 disruption (email alternatives, file access, emergency comms)?
  • What monitoring is in place to spot unusual remote sessions or new persistence on servers after an intrusion?
  • Given recent third-party breaches in professional services supply chains, what’s our process for tracking supplier incident notifications and assessing knock-on risk?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

If you run your own internet-facing services, today’s reporting highlights that widely used components (including web server and runtime stacks) can receive urgent fixes. Rather than rushing ad-hoc, ask your IT provider to confirm which public-facing services you operate, how quickly critical fixes are applied, and what monitoring is in place in case attackers got in before patching.

One action today

Send a 3-line staff alert: “Microsoft 365 issues are being reported. Don’t click ‘outage fix’ links, don’t approve unexpected MFA prompts, and only accept IT remote support via our usual verified process.”
