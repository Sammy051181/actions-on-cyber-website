Today’s SME cyber look-out: Microsoft sign-in phishing (AitM) and risky AI assistant permissions

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

Moderate Sunday 04 October 2026, 14:11 UK time
Today’s look-out: Credential theft via convincing Microsoft-style sign-in pages + AI/SaaS data-sharing and over-permission risk

What to look out for today

1) Convincing Microsoft-style sign-in phishing (AitM). A campaign reported today targets people with tailored emails and aims to capture credentials and sign-in session tokens using “adversary-in-the-middle” (AitM) techniques.

2) AI assistant data and permission creep. Two separate themes are in the news: prompts asking users to share voice conversations for AI training, and AI assistants moving towards broader access to files/apps/web actions on Macs.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

  • Stolen sign-ins can bypass MFA. If attackers grab session tokens, they may get into email and cloud apps even when MFA is enabled, leading to invoice fraud, payroll changes, or mailbox rules that hide replies.
  • AI tooling can quietly increase data exposure. “Opt-in” training prompts and broad device/file permissions can create accidental leakage of client data, student data, HR info, legal material, or commercially sensitive documents.
  • Supplier/SaaS risk is real for SMEs. Even if you’re not a target “sector”, attackers reuse the same phishing and credential-stealing playbooks across organisations of all sizes.

Warning signs

  • Email invitations or messages that push you to sign in urgently (policy update, document share, account warning), especially if they’re unusually personalised.
  • Sign-in pages that look correct but arrive via a link you didn’t expect (including links from shared documents or third-party mail platforms).
  • Unexpected prompts in AI tools asking to share voice recordings, “help improve the model”, or enable broader access to content.
  • Requests to let an AI assistant access files/apps or perform actions “without asking every time”.
  • Users reporting they are repeatedly asked to re-authenticate, or seeing unusual login prompts after clicking a link.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • AitM phishing: attackers lure staff to a realistic sign-in flow and capture both credentials and session tokens, then access email or cloud services to steal data or commit payment diversion.
  • Impersonation and pretexting: messages may impersonate credible figures or industry contacts to increase trust and reduce scrutiny.
  • AI permission misuse: if staff enable broad AI assistant access, sensitive documents could be exposed through misconfiguration, oversharing, or simple user error (e.g., uploading client material into the wrong place).

What to do today

  • Remind staff: don’t sign in from links in unexpected emails. Instead, open Microsoft 365/Google Workspace via a saved bookmark or your normal app.
  • Confirm your policy on AI tools: whether staff may share voice conversations for training, and what categories of business data must never be uploaded/shared.
  • Review AI assistant permissions on company Macs: limit file/app access to what’s necessary; avoid “always allow” for business accounts unless there’s a clear business case and approval.
  • Prioritise mailbox and payment controls: dual approval for supplier bank detail changes and urgent payments; watch for new inbox rules/forwarding.

Ask your IT provider

  • Do we have phishing-resistant sign-in protections in place (e.g., conditional access, token theft mitigations), and do we alert on unusual sign-ins?
  • Can you report on new email forwarding rules, suspicious mailbox rule creation, and impossible travel / unusual login locations?
  • Do we have an agreed approved AI tools list and a way to control/monitor business accounts used with AI services?
  • For Macs, do we have guidance or management controls for app permissions and data access tied to AI assistants?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

If you (or a supplier/IT partner) use Dell Container Storage Modules (CSM) on Kubernetes, check whether you are affected by the newly reported Dell CSM security flaws and ensure updates are planned. This is mainly relevant to organisations running their own Kubernetes storage stack (more common in larger IT environments or specialist providers than in typical SMEs).

One action today

Send a same-day staff note: “Don’t sign in from links in unexpected emails—use your saved Microsoft 365/Google bookmark—and don’t approve new AI voice/data sharing prompts without checking with your manager/IT.”
