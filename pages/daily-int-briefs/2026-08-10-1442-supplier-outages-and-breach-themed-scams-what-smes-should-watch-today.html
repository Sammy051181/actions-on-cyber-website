Supplier outages and breach-themed scams: what SMEs should watch today

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Monday 10 August 2026, 14:42 UK time
Today’s look-out: Supplier incident scams and third‑party service disruption

What to look out for today

  • Supplier breach ripple effects: Valve is notifying European Steam hardware customers after a breach at its shipping partner (CEVA Logistics). Expect criminals to reuse this theme for phishing and fake “delivery problem” messages.
  • Third-party hosted service disruption: LexisNexis took some services offline after suspicious activity on servers run by an unnamed third-party vendor. If your firm uses LexisNexis products (e.g. due diligence, monitoring, research), plan for short-notice outages and knock-on workflow disruption.
  • “Phishing-resistant” login hype risk: New research shows ways passkey protections can be undermined in certain scenarios (often when malware is already on a device or where authentication material is exposed). Don’t treat passkeys as a magic shield—device security still matters.
  • Internet-facing appliance risk (managed by IT/MSP): Progress Kemp LoadMaster is being actively exploited in attacks, per CISA warnings. This is mainly relevant if you (or your provider) run LoadMaster for load balancing/VPN/app publishing.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

SMEs are increasingly dependent on third parties—logistics providers, research platforms, and cloud-hosted tools. When a supplier is breached or takes systems offline, criminals often follow up with targeted scams (fake delivery fees, password resets, invoice changes) while teams are distracted. Separately, “secure login” improvements like passkeys reduce some phishing, but they do not remove the need for strong endpoint protection and good incident response.

Warning signs

  • Email/SMS claiming to be from a delivery firm or retailer asking you to pay a small fee, “rebook delivery”, or open a link to “confirm address”.
  • Unexpected “your account was affected” messages that push you to log in urgently or call a phone number.
  • Staff reporting they can’t access LexisNexis tools they rely on (sudden outage, login failures, API errors) and are asked to use an “alternative” link.
  • Unusual authentication prompts or sign-in notifications, especially after a user clicks a link or installs “updated software”.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • Breach-themed phishing: Using real news (shipping partner breach; service shutdown) to make fake support emails and “security verification” pages more believable.
  • Business email compromise follow-up: If a supplier breach exposes contact details, criminals may try invoice redirection or payment diversion by impersonating a supplier or logistics partner.
  • Malware first, account takeover second: Research on passkeys highlights a key point for SMEs: if a device is compromised, attackers can sometimes defeat otherwise strong authentication workflows.
  • Targeting exposed edge systems: Where organisations run internet-facing appliances (like load balancers), active exploitation can lead to remote access, downtime, or a stepping stone into the network.

What to do today

  • Brief staff (5 minutes): “Be cautious with delivery/payment/security emails this week; don’t click links—use saved bookmarks or official apps; report anything urgent-looking.”
  • Confirm your ‘outage plan’: If your team uses LexisNexis services, identify a manual fallback (who can approve work, alternative data sources, what to do if deadlines are impacted).
  • Tighten payment controls: Any bank detail change should require a call-back to a known number (from your records, not the email) and ideally dual approval.
  • Endpoint hygiene: Ensure devices have active EDR/AV, disk encryption, and that staff can’t install random software “updates”. Passkeys help, but compromised devices can still be abused.

Ask your IT provider

  • Do we run Progress Kemp LoadMaster anywhere (on-prem or hosted), and if so, is it monitored and being actively managed in response to the current exploitation reports?
  • What’s our process for third-party incident comms—who decides whether we warn staff/clients, and how quickly can we do it?
  • Are passkeys enabled for key accounts, and what extra protections do we have for device compromise (EDR coverage, admin rights control, alerting)?
  • Can you provide a list of our critical suppliers/SaaS tools and the “what we do if it’s down” plan for each?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

If you use Progress Kemp LoadMaster, treat today’s “actively exploited” reporting as a prompt to confirm your provider is on top of vendor guidance and monitoring. This isn’t about panic patching—it’s about verifying ownership: who manages it, how you know it’s updated, and what alerts you’ll receive if it’s targeted.

One action today

Send a short internal note: “No one pays delivery fees or changes supplier bank details from an email—use bookmarks/known numbers and report anything urgent-looking to IT/finance.”
