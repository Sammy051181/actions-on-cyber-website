Today’s SMB Cyber Brief: “Ransomware recovery” impersonation scams, AI/SaaS disruption, and breach-driven phishing

What small and medium-sized businesses should look out for today.

High Thursday 20 August 2026, 08:55 UK time
Today’s look-out: Supplier incident scams and urgent-payment fraud during ransomware and SaaS disruption

What to look out for today

1) “Ransomware recovery” impostors chasing payments. A suspected ransomware affiliate is reportedly posing as a recovery firm (“Ransom Busters”), contacting victims early and offering decryption keys / data deletion for a fee.

2) SaaS dependency disruption (ChatGPT login/sign-up outage). If your teams rely on ChatGPT for customer comms, drafting, research, support scripts, or internal workflow, plan for short-notice downtime.

3) Breach ripple-effects. Large breach disclosures (e.g., healthtech and cloud providers) often trigger follow-on scams: fake “security notices”, password reset lures, and invoice/payment diversion attempts using believable context.

Why this matters to smaller businesses

  • Ransomware events are chaotic. Attackers exploit urgency and confusion to get money sent quickly—sometimes to the wrong party.
  • SMEs frequently outsource recovery. That makes you a prime target for “helpful” inbound calls/emails claiming to be negotiators, recovery specialists, or your insurer’s partner.
  • Outages hit productivity. When AI/SaaS tools go down, staff may switch to personal accounts, new tools, or forwarding data externally—creating data leakage and compliance risk.

Warning signs

  • Unsolicited outreach claiming they can decrypt files or delete stolen data if you pay them.
  • Requests to pay quickly by crypto, “urgent bank transfer”, or to a new payee not previously used.
  • Someone references your incident before you’ve publicly disclosed it, and pressures you to keep it secret or bypass your IT provider/insurer.
  • During AI/SaaS outages: staff asking to use personal emails, personal AI accounts, or copy/paste customer data into “another tool”.
  • Post-breach themed emails/texts: “Your account was in the breach—log in here”, “Update payroll/bank details”, “New secure portal”.

How attackers may exploit the situation

  • Double-dip extortion: criminals involved in (or aware of) ransomware incidents impersonate a recovery service to collect a separate fee.
  • Payment diversion: they may introduce new bank details under the guise of “recovery costs”, “forensic retainers”, or “data deletion fees”.
  • Outage opportunism: when ChatGPT or other tools are unavailable, attackers push “alternative sign-in” or “fix your account” phishing, or staff adopt unapproved tools that expose data.
  • Credibility laundering: they use widely reported breach/outage news to make scams feel timely and legitimate.

What to do today

  • Set a rule: only your pre-approved incident-response channel can engage any “recovery”, “negotiation”, or “decryption” service—no exceptions.
  • Lock down payment changes: require call-back verification to a known number (not the email signature) before paying any new supplier or changing bank details.
  • Brief staff in 5 minutes: “If you receive an email/call offering ransomware help or asking for urgent payment, forward to IT/security/finance and do not respond.”
  • Prepare for AI/SaaS downtime: confirm what staff should do if ChatGPT is unavailable (approved alternatives, offline templates, and a reminder not to paste sensitive data into unapproved tools).
  • Log and preserve evidence: keep emails, numbers, and payment requests—useful if you need to involve your bank/insurer/police.

Ask your IT provider

  • What is our approved ransomware response workflow (including insurer, legal, comms, forensics), and who can authorise third-party engagement?
  • Do we have controls to prevent/flag new payees and urgent payment requests (especially out-of-hours)?
  • How do we monitor for lookalike domains and impersonation attempts during incidents?
  • What’s our policy on using AI tools (including what data is prohibited), and how is it enforced?
  • If a core SaaS tool is down, what is our business continuity plan for customer support, sales, and operations?

Patch watch - only one short paragraph, and only if relevant

No specific patch action is the key takeaway today. The main risk is impersonation and payment fraud during incidents, plus operational disruption when a relied-upon SaaS/AI tool is unavailable—focus on verification steps, comms discipline, and continuity planning.

One action today

Send a same-day internal note: “No one is authorised to engage ‘ransomware recovery’ services or approve urgent payments—route any such contact to IT/finance for call-back verification using known numbers.”
